# How an mRNA Lipid Nanoparticle Puts a Protein on the Cell Surface

**Dek:** A 34-second schematic follows one particle from blood to membrane. Here is the mechanism the picture compresses, the measurements it does not make, and the trafficking step it leaves out.

**Meta description:** How lipid nanoparticle mRNA delivery works: PEG-lipid corona, endocytosis, ionizable-lipid protonation, endosomal escape, ribosomal translation, and why a membrane antigen does not skip the secretory path.

**Slug:** mrna-lipid-nanoparticle-endosomal-escape

**Author line for Medium:** Miz Causevic. This is a mechanism essay about a schematic film, not a clinical claim and not a lab report.

---

An mRNA lipid nanoparticle has one job in this picture. Keep a strand intact long enough to reach a cell, get that strand into the cytosol, and hand it to a ribosome. If the encoded protein is a membrane antigen, the cell can later display it on the outer surface.

That is the entire 34-second film. Five beats, one particle, no scale bar that would pretend a ribosome and a cell surface belong in the same frame. The rest of this piece is what those beats are allowed to mean, and where the drawing is simpler than the cell.

I built the film as a Remotion composition at 1920×1080, 30 frames per second, 1,020 frames. The on-screen line is "schematic, not to scale." That line is the editorial constraint, not a caption afterthought.

## Circulation is a surface problem before it is an uptake problem

In blood, the particle has to avoid sticking to cells and proteins long enough to arrive. The film shows that as a mint shell and a halo of short chains. Those chains are the PEG-lipid corona: polyethylene glycol conjugated to a lipid anchor, drawn here as a thermal wobble rather than as a chemical structure.

Hou, Zaks, Langer, and Dong, in their 2021 review in *Nature Reviews Materials*, describe why that surface is engineered the way it is. Ionizable lipids are protonated, and therefore charged, at low pH. At physiological pH they stay closer to neutral, which reduces interaction with the anionic membranes of blood cells. PEG-lipids, in the same review, are there to limit aggregation and to slow clearance by the kidneys and the mononuclear phagocyte system. The length of the lipid anchor changes how long the PEG stays on the particle. Shorter anchors leave sooner. That is a formulation choice, not a visual I can show with five beads on a stalk.

The film does not show the other two usual components. A typical mRNA lipid nanoparticle also contains cholesterol and a helper phospholipid. Truong, Kafle, Nguyen, Li, and Meng, writing in *Journal of Nanobiotechnology* in April 2026, restate the clinical pattern: ionizable lipid, cholesterol, phospholipid, and a PEGylated lipid. Moderna’s and Pfizer-BioNTech’s vaccine formulations are the reference ratios in that paper, about 50 mol% and 46.3 mol% ionizable lipid. I am not animating those ratios. A schematic shell that reads as "ionizable lipid" in one color will lie if a viewer thinks the particle is made of one molecule.

## The cutaway is a teaching cut, not a structure

At five seconds the shell splits on the equator and a violet helix appears. That shot answers a layout question: where is the payload relative to the shell? It does not answer a structural question.

mRNA inside a formulated particle is complexed with ionizable lipid. It is not a free spring sitting in an empty cavity, waiting for a hemisphere to lift off. I drew it as a helix because a dense coil, seen in a 34-second commercial, reads as a single strand. The cut is a jig. The rings and posts around the particle are a viewing fixture. They are not biology. If a still from that second gets reused without the "schematic" line, it will be misread as a cryo-EM model. It is not one.

## Uptake, acid, and the step most of the particles miss

After the section closes, the particle meets a bilayer. The membrane dips, a neck tightens, and a vesicle pinches off. That is endocytosis, drawn as one route. Hou and colleagues note that lipid nanoparticles can enter by macropinocytosis and by clathrin-mediated and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, and that the route depends on the particle and the cell. The film picks the cup-and-pinch picture because it is the one a viewer can follow in seven seconds. It is one route, not the inventory.

Inside the endosome the pH falls. The on-screen meter is labeled neutral at the top and acidic at the bottom. It has no number, because the film is not an instrument. The literature range people actually argue about is roughly pH 5 to 6 in the late endosome, which Truong and colleagues use when they describe protonation. Chatterjee, Kon, Sharma, and Peer, in a 2024 *PNAS* perspective, are more careful than a single pH: the compartment where escape happens is still argued, and early-versus-late endosome markers do not settle it.

Protonation is the point of an ionizable lipid. At low pH the headgroup takes on a charge. Hou and colleagues write that this can promote membrane destabilization and escape. Chatterjee and colleagues separate two theories and do not pretend the field has picked a winner.

One theory: protonated ionizable lipids meet anionic lipids on the inside of the endosomal membrane, the pair favors a non-bilayer inverted hexagonal phase, and the membrane fails. The other: the proton sponge. Buffering pulls in protons and counterions, water follows, the vesicle swells, and it bursts. Both are in the *PNAS* piece. Both have supporting and criticizing papers behind them. The film shows the observable the brief asked for: the shell brightens, the vesicle opens, the strand leaves. It does not animate hexagonal phase, chloride flux, or osmotic rupture as separate physics. Those would be different shots, and they would still be hypotheses.

Escape is also the step the particle usually fails. Gilleron and colleagues, imaging siRNA lipid nanoparticles in cells and mouse liver, estimated cytosolic escape at 1 to 2 percent, and only during a short window in a compartment with both early and late endosome features (*Nature Biotechnology*, 2013). Chatterjee and colleagues, in the 2024 *PNAS* perspective, still treat that low yield as the bottleneck for LNP therapeutics and note that much of the material is recycled or held toward lysosomal degradation. That 1 to 2 percent is an siRNA imaging estimate. It is not a measurement of the mRNA particle in this film, and it is not a yield I filmed. The honest use of the number is this: a schematic that shows one particle escaping cleanly will teach the path and hide the yield.

## The ribosome does not get the strand for free

Once the strand is drawn in the cytosol, the film feeds it through a two-subunit ribosome and grows an amber peptide that then collapses into a compact cluster. That is decoding and chain compaction, squeezed into eight seconds.

Truong and colleagues add a step the film only implies. Cytosolic location is not translation. The mRNA still has to come off the ionizable lipid. In their 2026 study they varied ionizable lipid content, using SM-102 and ALC-0315, and reported that formulations near 30 mol% ionizable lipid transfected better in their in vitro assays and expressed more in mice than the higher, clinically familiar ratios, even when uptake and escape looked similar. Their colocalization images are the evidence they offer for greater dissociation at the lower ratio. Scope that correctly. It is one paper, two ionizable lipids, cell assays plus mice. It is not a reason to redraw every approved formulation, and it is not something the film measured. It is the reason the violet strand in the shot is shown leaving the shell before it enters the ribosome, instead of being translated while still buried in lipid.

The peptide "fold" in the film is a spatial collapse of beads. Real folding is chemistry: backbone angles, hydrophobic collapse, disulfide bonds, chaperones, and, for many proteins, help from the endoplasmic reticulum. The cluster is a sign that the chain is no longer a straight oligomer. It is not a structure prediction.

## A membrane antigen does not teleport to the surface

The last shot pulls back through the bilayer and plants folded amber proteins on the outside. That is the picture people ask for when they say "the cell displays the antigen." It is also the picture most likely to be wrong if you read it literally.

A protein that anchors in the plasma membrane, spike being the familiar vaccine example, is not built on a free cytosolic ribosome and then pushed into the outer bilayer from the cytosol. It is translated at the rough endoplasmic reticulum, threaded in as it is made, folded and glycosylated there, and sent through the Golgi to the surface. Cytosolic ribosomes make cytosolic proteins. The film puts the ribosome in the cytosol because that is the readable version of "mRNA meets the decoding center," then cuts to surface proteins because that is the readable version of "antigen on the membrane." The cut deletes the secretory path.

I would rather label the deletion than decorate the film with a tiny ER nobody can read at 30 frames per second. If you use a still from the last eight seconds in a lecture, say the sentence the picture cannot: membrane display of a transmembrane antigen runs through the secretory pathway. The amber lollipops are the destination, not the route.

The population of proteins is also a compression. One strand, one ribosome, then many copies on the surface. Cells do make many copies from one mRNA, and many particles hit one cell. The film does not count them. There is no expression number, no titer, and no claim that display equals protection.

## What I refused to put on screen

A kinetic science film fails in a specific way. It looks measured. Timecode, a pH bar, a section jig, and a clean palette all borrow the authority of an instrument. So the rules for this one were strict.

No numeric pH. The bar moves from a neutral label to an acidic label.

No fake nanometer scale. A lipid nanoparticle, an endosome, a ribosome, and a cell surface do not share a zoom in real space. Forcing them into one continuous camera is the point of the schematic and the reason the disclaimer stays up.

No efficacy sentence. Escape on screen is not immunogenicity. Truong and colleagues measured antibody and T-cell responses in their mice for their formulations. This film did not.

No product identity. The shell is not SM-102, not ALC-0315, and not a specific vaccine. Those names belong in papers that formulated them. Here the mint is "ionizable lipid" as a class behavior: nearer neutral in blood, charged when the vesicle acidifies.

No invented author for the biology. The mechanism sentences above are tied to Hou 2021, Chatterjee 2024, Truong 2026, and the siRNA imaging papers those reviews already rely on. Where those sources disagree, the disagreement stays in the paragraph. The hexagonal-phase account and the proton-sponge account are both still in print.

## How the picture is built, for the person who has to change it

The film is one Remotion composition, `LnpDelivery`, in a repository called `lnp-mrna-anatomy`. React draws every frame. Three.js, through `@remotion/three`, draws the particle, the bilayer, the vesicle, the ribosome, and the surface proteins. Camera moves are keyed in `src/timeline.ts`. Nothing in the scene calls `Math.random()` during a frame. Positions that look scattered were seeded once, so frame 420 is the same picture on every render.

That determinism is the production requirement. A scientific schematic you cannot re-render exactly is a poster you cannot defend.

Color is doing classification, not decoration.

- Cytosol `#0B0F19`
- Bilayer `#1F2A44`
- Ionizable lipid and the protonation accent `#00F5D4`
- mRNA `#9D4EDD`
- Ribosome and antigen `#FFB703`

The type is Inter and JetBrains Mono. The five-step index at the top is the table of contents. The lower third is the sentence for that beat. A single luminance dip at about 25 seconds covers the camera crossing from inside the cell to outside it. One dip, not a flicker. The pH bar fills once. It does not pulse.

If you fork the repo for a different payload, change the strand color, the lower-third nouns, and the last object on the membrane. Do not keep the word "antigen" if the encoded product is a cytosolic enzyme. The last shot would be a different destination, and the secretory-path caveat above would no longer apply.

## Questions the stills provoke

**Does the particle open in the blood?**
No. The equatorial split between 5 and 10 seconds is a section cut for the viewer. The shell closes again before the membrane cups it. In the mechanism, the particle is intact in circulation.

**Is endosomal escape solved?**
No. The vesicle opens in the film because that is the successful path. Chatterjee and colleagues treat escape as an unsolved bottleneck, with two live theories and a cytosolic yield, drawn from earlier siRNA imaging, under 2%.

**Why is the ribosome in the cytosol if the protein ends up in the membrane?**
Because the film separates "mRNA is read" from "protein is displayed," and it does not have time to draw the endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi. For a transmembrane antigen, those organelles are not optional. They are the route.

**Can I use a frame as a figure in a methods section?**
Only as a schematic, with the scale line and a citation to a real methods paper for anything quantitative. A frame from this render is not data.

**What would the next shot be, if the film were longer?**
Dissociation of mRNA from ionizable lipid as its own beat, then signal recognition and the translocon, then a Golgi pass, then arrival at the surface. Escape yield would be a caption, not a hero particle. The current cut is the path. It is not the success rate.
